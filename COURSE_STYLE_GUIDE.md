# AI Course Generation Style Guide

## Lesson Length and Scope

Each lesson should take approximately 20–30 minutes to complete, including reading, examples, activities, and knowledge checks.

A lesson should have a focused purpose. When the planned learning outcomes cannot be taught adequately within 30 minutes, divide the material into multiple lessons rather than compressing or oversimplifying it.

Each lesson should normally contain one primary learning outcome and no more than two or three closely related supporting outcomes.

## Narrative Structure

Courses should be written more like textbooks than study guides. Lessons should not read as disconnected lists of definitions, facts, or concepts.

Each lesson should follow a clear narrative progression:

1. Introduce the topic and explain why it matters.
2. Connect it to relevant ideas the learner already knows.
3. Explain the new material in a logical sequence.
4. Use examples to make abstract ideas concrete.
5. Give the learner an opportunity to apply the material.
6. Summarize the main ideas and connect them to the next lesson.

Headings, lists, tables, and callout boxes may be used to improve clarity, but they should support the lesson’s narrative rather than replace it.

## Vocabulary and Terminology

Do not introduce unfamiliar vocabulary without explanation.

Define a new term when it first appears. The definition may be included directly in the text or made available through a hover definition, glossary link, or similar interface element.

Definitions should use plain language and should not depend on additional unexplained terminology.

After defining a term, demonstrate how it is used in context. Avoid introducing many new terms in the same paragraph.

Use terminology consistently throughout the course. Do not alternate between different terms for the same concept unless the distinction is explained.

## Explanations and Examples

Explain both what something is and why it matters.

Move from simple ideas to more complex ones. Do not assume that a learner understands a concept merely because it was mentioned earlier.

Use examples throughout the lesson rather than placing all examples at the end. Examples should closely match the concept being explained and should be detailed enough for the learner to follow the reasoning.

When presenting a process, show the individual steps and explain the purpose of each step.

When presenting an abstract concept, include at least one concrete example, analogy, scenario, diagram, or demonstration.

When spatial relationships, quantitative comparisons, trends, systems, or sequences are central to the learning outcome, show them as a map, chart, or diagram instead of describing the entire visual in prose. Explain how to read the visual, then ask the learner to draw a conclusion from evidence it contains.

Maps should use authoritative base geography. Charts should expose their underlying values and labels. Visuals must include meaningful captions and alt text, and activities or knowledge checks should reference the visual directly when interpretation is part of the outcome.

## Learner Assumptions

State any required prior knowledge at the beginning of the course or lesson.

Do not assume specialized knowledge unless it has been listed as a prerequisite or taught earlier in the course.

Briefly remind learners of earlier concepts when those concepts are necessary to understand new material.

## Activities and Knowledge Checks

Activities should directly support the stated learning outcomes. Avoid exercises that test details that were not taught or that are unrelated to the lesson’s main purpose.

Place brief knowledge checks after important sections rather than relying only on an assessment at the end.

Feedback should explain why an answer is correct or incorrect. It should help the learner correct their understanding rather than simply reveal the right answer.

The expected time required for activities and assessments should be included when estimating lesson length.

## Tone and Readability

Use clear, direct language. Prefer familiar words and straightforward sentence structures.

Write for the intended learner level without sounding childish, overly academic, or unnecessarily technical.

Use paragraphs to develop ideas. Avoid excessive bullet points, fragments, and dense blocks of text.

Vary sentence and paragraph length to maintain a natural reading rhythm.

Use second person when giving instructions to the learner, but avoid repetitive phrases such as “you will learn” or “in this lesson” when the meaning is already clear.

## Lesson Openings and Conclusions

Begin each lesson with a brief explanation of what the learner will be able to understand or do by the end. Frame the topic around a meaningful question, problem, or practical use.

End each lesson with a concise summary of the main ideas. The conclusion should reinforce the learning outcome and, when appropriate, explain how the lesson connects to what comes next.

## Accuracy and Completeness

Do not invent facts, sources, statistics, quotations, or examples presented as real events.

When information is uncertain, disputed, or likely to change, clearly qualify it.

Do not omit necessary explanations merely to meet the target lesson length. Reduce the lesson’s scope or divide it into multiple lessons instead.
